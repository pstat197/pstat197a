Boundaries of the analysis

  • describe the respondents represented in the relevant files

  • demographic summaries cannot be linked to individual skills or preferences

  • self-rated proficiency and comfort measure students’ perceptions

  • an observed association does not establish a cause

Descriptive analysis can include modeling. Explore models that help answer your questions, and interpret their results in terms of these respondents.

Modeling as descriptive analysis

In class, we used k-means clustering on proficiency and comfort ratings to explore profiles of respondents.

  • you are encouraged to extend that analysis or try another modeling approach

  • a model can summarize several variables together or reveal patterns worth examining

  • explain what the model adds to your question and how you interpret its output

Exploratory modeling is part of this assignment’s scope. Its conclusions should remain descriptive.

Starting with the example coursework question

Do students who have taken PSTAT 131 and PSTAT 134 rate themselves higher in programming proficiency?

Before analyzing, your group needs to decide:

  • does the comparison mean both courses versus everyone else, or both, one, and neither?

  • what would higher self-rated proficiency look like in the data?

  • any additional question(s) that might help you understand the pattern?

Developing the coursework question

  • identify completion of each course from courses in background-clean.csv

  • compare the distribution of prog.prof across the coursework groups, showing group sizes

  • consider whether programming comfort shows a similar pattern

  • optionally explore a model relating proficiency to coursework and other preparation

Describe the association you observe. These data cannot establish that taking the courses caused higher proficiency.

Check the actual course labels before coding indicators. Keep missing course responses distinct from confirmed non-selection. Proficiency is ordered beginner/intermediate/advanced. An ordinal model is one possible extension, subject to checking the small sample and category counts.

What adds useful complexity?

  • compare distributions rather than reporting just one average

  • compare the same respondents across skill areas

  • examine combinations of interests or preferences

  • use a model to examine several variables together or summarize respondent profiles

Each additional step should help answer the question. Check group sizes before adding more subdivisions.

Possible directions

Use these as starting points if your group is stuck:

  • how do programming language preferences differ across respondents’ programming proficiency levels and completed coursework?

  • which application domains tend to be selected together? do those combinations differ between respondents who prefer industry versus research projects?

  • how do respondents with and without a domain specialization differ in the breadth and types of data science interests they select?

  • which application domains commonly appear together in respondents’ selections?

These are prompts, not assigned questions. Encourage groups to explain what they would learn and identify an extension beyond summaries already shown in class.

Extending the clustering example

What makes the respondent profiles distinct, and how much do they depend on our choices?

  • compare clusters based on proficiency alone, comfort alone, or both

  • try a few values of \(k\) and describe how the profiles change

  • examine how scaling the variables affects the grouping

  • summarize coursework or project preferences within the resulting clusters

Students should explain cluster sizes and profiles, not just show a colored scatterplot. Coding proficiency as 1/2/3 assumes equal spacing, and the comfort ratings use a different scale. Scaling and variable choices change the distances used by k-means. A difference in a variable used to form clusters is expected by construction; variables not used to form them provide additional descriptive context. Do not frame one clustering as the discovery of definitive student types.
