Allow approximately 5–6 minutes at the start of class for announcements and general questions. The timed activities below total 69 minutes.

What are we creating?

A method vignette is a simple, reproducible example that helps someone learn a data science method or tool.

Imagine a student encountering your topic during next term’s capstone project:

  • what would they need to understand first?
  • what could they learn by working through your example?
  • what should they be able to do afterward?

Think of a starting point for learning, not a comprehensive survey of a subject.

What your topic needs to support

Your final public repository should include:

  1. A README.md introducing the vignette, contributors, contents, and at least two references for further learning
  2. A primary vignette explaining the method and walking through implementation step by step
  3. At least one example dataset
  4. An annotated script reproducing the results in the vignette end-to-end

The vignette should read more like a lab activity than a research paper.

Two topics that sound similar may differ in scope

Broad: Neural networks for text analysis

More focused: Using a pretrained text embedding model to represent documents and compare their similarity

Broad: Geospatial analysis

More focused: Reading spatial polygons in R and performing a spatial join with point observations

A strong topic gives you something specific to explain, demonstrate, and reproduce with example data.

These are illustrative ways to narrow scope, not required topics. Students may work in R, Python, or another suitable environment. They should not assume a sophisticated algorithm is automatically a better choice.

Individual brainstorming

On your own: 4 minutes

Write down 2–3 possible topics, including ideas you brought to class.

For each, jot down:

  • a method or tool you would teach
  • one concrete task it could help someone accomplish
  • why you think another capstone student might find it useful

Keep the ideas tentative for now.

Pooling and narrowing ideas

With your group: 6 minutes

  1. Take turns sharing ideas; make sure everyone contributes.
  2. Combine overlapping ideas where appropriate.
  3. Select 2–3 candidates to investigate more closely.

Favor ideas that interest the group and could become a useful, self-contained tutorial.

From topic to learning goal

A topic name alone is not a teaching plan.

For each candidate, complete these sentences:

  • Our vignette will teach someone how to …
  • We will demonstrate this by …
  • After following it, a reader should be able to …

Try to name the actual operation or workflow a reader will learn—not only the field in which it is used.

Develop your candidates

With your group: 10 minutes

For each of your 2–3 candidates, sketch:

  1. The specific method, tool, or workflow to teach
  2. A realistic example question or task
  3. The type of data needed to demonstrate it
  4. The major implementation steps a reader would follow
  5. One concept or common mistake you would need to explain

Result: a few sentences outlining a possible vignette for each idea.

What might a reader actually do?

Example: Anomaly detection in time series

  • read and visualize a time-indexed dataset
  • select and apply one detection method
  • examine identified anomalies in the original series
  • change a tuning choice and compare the results
  • discuss what the method can and cannot establish

A good walkthrough makes the important choices visible, rather than simply running a package function once.

This is a hypothetical vignette outline. It is not meant to prescribe a method, dataset, or particular package.

Find a starting point

With your group: 10 minutes

For your strongest two candidates, search for:

  • a reliable tutorial, vignette, textbook section, or documentation page
  • an example dataset you could use or adapt
  • code, package documentation, or an implementation

Record links and describe each resource.

Question: Is there enough material to teach the topic accurately, and a manageable example you can make your own?

Using existing vignettes responsibly

You may closely follow another tutorial or vignette if you cite it and either:

  • apply the approach to different data, or
  • do something new with the method or workflow

You may not assemble a collage of copied sources or simply rewrite an existing vignette.

Finding your own example data is often a good way to ensure you must understand and adapt the code.

Ask groups to keep their candidate references. We will briefly review references for sufficient originality, as discussed in class.

Feasibility and scope check

With your group: 9 minutes

Evaluate each leading candidate:

  • Teaching value: What useful new skill will a reader gain?
  • Specificity: Can you cover it coherently in a short, step-by-step example?
  • Data: Can you access an appropriate dataset and share it in the repository?
  • Implementation: Can your group run and explain the workflow end-to-end?
  • Originality: How will it differ from the references you found?
  • Time: Can you prepare a draft by November 30?

Identify the main uncertainty or risk for each candidate.

When an idea is too ambitious

Rather than attempting to teach an entire family of methods, consider limiting:

  • the method: one approach rather than several
  • the task: one use case rather than many
  • the data: one accessible example dataset
  • the workflow: a few steps that can be explained and reproduced

You can mention advanced variations or related methods as next steps without implementing them all.

Try the first step

With your group: 10 minutes

For your preferred candidate, attempt one small, concrete step:

  • locate and inspect a potential example dataset, or
  • install/load a relevant package and run a minimal example, or
  • trace an existing tutorial and identify what you would need to adapt

Write down what worked, what you still need to learn, and whether this changes your topic’s scope.

You do not need to produce a working vignette today. This is an early reality check.

A second pair of eyes

Exchange ideas with another group: 8 minutes

Each group: briefly describe your two leading candidates and the proposed examples.

The other group should ask:

  • What would I know how to do after following this vignette?
  • Which step would be hardest for a beginner to understand?
  • Is the example concrete enough to reproduce?
  • Does the scope sound reasonable for the available time?

Take notes on feedback that changes how you would frame either topic.

Select one topic

With your group: 7 minutes

Choose one topic for your vignette.

Agree on:

  1. A specific working title
  2. The main skill or workflow you will teach
  3. The example data or type of data you intend to use
  4. One or two promising references
  5. Your first concrete step toward a working example

If your preferred topic has a major unresolved obstacle, choose the more feasible alternative or narrow its scope.

Email your topic

With your group: 5 minutes

Send one email per group by the end of today, Thursday, November 19.

Include:

  • group members and working topic/title
  • 1–2 sentences describing what the vignette will teach
  • the planned example data or application
  • references or resources you have identified so far

The topic can be refined as you build the vignette; make your proposed focus clear enough for me to review.

The details above are a suggested structure for communicating the topic, not new grading requirements. Have groups send the email before leaving if possible. Allocate any remaining moments to immediate questions.
